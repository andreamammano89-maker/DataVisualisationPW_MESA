Introduction

From sharing economy to commercial market

Airbnb launched as a peer-to-peer idea: spare rooms, occasional guests, local hosts. That idea has not disappeared, but in many cities it now coexists with something quite different. A small number of professional operators manage large portfolios of listings, running what amounts to a commercial hotel business through the platform. The question is how far that shift has gone, and what it means for the neighbourhoods where it is most concentrated.

This project compares two European cities that sit at opposite ends of that spectrum. Rome is a high-intensity tourism market with fragmented regulation, where Airbnb has grown largely unchecked. Copenhagen operates under stricter housing rules and shows a very different pattern. Together they offer a controlled lens for examining how regulatory context shapes market structure and, ultimately, urban pressure.

The analysis combines host-level concentration metrics with a neighbourhood-level Urban Pressure Index (UPI) that captures listing density and year-round availability. The goal is not just to describe who is on the platform, but to understand where their activity lands spatially and what that means for the city.

This site is aimed at urban policymakers and city administrators who need data-driven evidence to assess short-term rental markets and evaluate regulatory interventions, and at journalists and informed members of the public interested in the housing and tourism dynamics behind Airbnb's growth.

Analytical Tasks

T1

Assess market concentration

How are listings distributed across hosts? Does a small number of operators control a disproportionate share?

T2

Classify host types

Distinguish casual (1 listing), semi-professional (2 to 4), and professional hosts (5 or more) and measure their market share.

T3

Compare cities

Identify structural differences between Rome and Copenhagen across price, availability, and host concentration.

T4

Analyse spatial distribution

Where are listings and professional hosts geographically concentrated within each city?

T5

Evaluate urban pressure

Measure neighbourhood-level intensity through a composite Urban Pressure Index combining listing density and availability.

Data

Two cities, one platform

Both datasets come from Inside Airbnb, a public transparency project that scrapes and publishes Airbnb listing data. The September 2025 snapshots were selected to ensure temporal comparability. Listings with missing prices, duplicate entries, and extreme price outliers above the 99th percentile were removed. Copenhagen prices, originally in Danish Krone, were converted to EUR at the September 2025 exchange rate of 1 DKK = 0.134 EUR.

One important data quality issue shapes the analysis throughout: approximately 41% of Copenhagen listings had no price recorded, compared to around 11% in Rome. Rows with missing prices were dropped from both datasets, which may introduce a selection bias in Copenhagen. For this reason, price was excluded from the Urban Pressure Index to preserve cross-city comparability.

Urban Pressure Index

A composite metric combining two min-max normalised components, computed separately within each city so the index captures relative pressure within each urban context rather than absolute differences between cities:

UPI = 0.5 x densitynorm + 0.5 x availabilitynorm

Equal weights reflect a neutral methodological assumption. Price was excluded due to missing data asymmetry across cities. A robustness check including price produces similar neighbourhood rankings.

Ethics

White hat and black hat

The same dataset can tell very different stories depending on design choices. The two visualisations below present the same relationship between professional host share and urban pressure. Toggle between them to see how easily a chart can mislead without falsifying a single data point.

White hat: honest representation
Scatter plot with confidence intervals, correlation statistics, and a causal disclaimer · Both cities
Why this is white hat: Both cities are shown simultaneously, preventing selective presentation of the stronger result. Both axes are anchored at zero. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals are shaded around each regression line, making uncertainty visible. Pearson correlation coefficients and p-values are annotated directly on the chart. The caption states explicitly that correlation does not imply causation and identifies the UPI as an author-constructed composite rather than an official statistic.

Findings

Key insights

Taken together, the data tell a consistent story. Rome has undergone a substantial commercialisation of its Airbnb market. Copenhagen has not. The gap is visible in every metric: concentration, availability, host composition, and spatial pressure.

1

Rome's market is highly concentrated among a small operator class

Professional hosts represent just 5.1% of all Rome hosts but control 29.4% of listings. The top 10% of hosts account for 39.2% of the entire market, and the top 1% alone control 13.9%. Rome's Gini coefficient of 0.39 confirms a structurally unequal distribution, visible in the Lorenz curve's pronounced deviation from the equality line.

Note on the HHI: the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index returns very low values in both cities (Rome: 0.0003, Copenhagen: 0.0005). This does not mean the markets are unconcentrated. The HHI is structurally insensitive in platform markets with thousands of small hosts, each holding a negligible individual share. The Gini coefficient and top-host dominance figures are more informative measures here.

2

Copenhagen remains close to the peer-to-peer model

96% of Copenhagen hosts manage a single listing, and casual hosts control 85.3% of all supply. The top 10% of hosts account for just 20.0% of listings, and the top 1% control 9.1%. The Gini coefficient of 0.11 reflects a broadly equitable distribution. Copenhagen's platform still functions largely as originally intended.

3

Availability patterns reveal commercial intensity in Rome

Rome's median availability of 260 days per year against 115 in Copenhagen points to near-permanent commercial operation for a large share of listings. 68.1% of Rome listings are available for six months or more, compared to 38.4% in Copenhagen. These listings are unlikely to represent occasional home-sharing.

4

Urban pressure clusters in historic and central districts

In Rome, the highest UPI scores and professional host shares concentrate in I Centro Storico, where professional hosts control 38.8% of listings. In Copenhagen, Indre By and Vesterbro show the most relative pressure, though at considerably lower absolute intensity. Pressure is spatially selective, not city-wide.

5

Professional hosting and urban pressure co-occur spatially

Neighbourhoods with higher professional host shares also tend to show higher UPI scores. The Pearson correlation is 0.55 in Rome (p = 0.035) and 0.73 in Copenhagen (p = 0.010). This is not evidence of causation. Tourism demand, urban centrality, and housing structure may drive both simultaneously. But the spatial co-occurrence is consistent and statistically significant.

Limitations

Missing prices

41% of Copenhagen listings had no price recorded. Dropping these rows may introduce selection bias and limits price-based comparisons between cities.

Single snapshot

Both datasets reflect September 2025 only. Seasonal patterns in availability and pricing are not captured, and the picture may differ at other times of year.

No housing context

The UPI measures Airbnb activity intensity, not housing displacement directly. Population density and residential housing supply data would be needed to support causal claims.

Platform data only

Inside Airbnb captures only publicly listed properties. Unlisted or off-platform short-term rentals are not reflected, potentially underestimating true market scale.
