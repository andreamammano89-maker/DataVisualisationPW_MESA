Introduction

From sharing economy to commercial market

Airbnb launched as a peer-to-peer idea: spare rooms, occasional guests, local hosts. That original model has not disappeared, but in many cities it now coexists with something quite different. A small number of professional operators manage large portfolios of listings, running what amounts to a commercial hotel business through the platform. This shift matters for policymakers because it changes who benefits from short-term rentals, and at what cost to ordinary residents.

This project compares two European cities that sit at opposite ends of that spectrum. Rome is a high-intensity tourism market with fragmented regulation, where Airbnb has grown with little constraint. Copenhagen operates under stricter housing rules and shows a very different pattern. Comparing them reveals how much regulatory context shapes market structure and, ultimately, urban pressure.

The analysis combines host-level concentration metrics with a neighbourhood-level Urban Pressure Index (UPI) that captures listing density and year-round availability. The goal is not just to describe who is on the platform, but to show where their activity concentrates spatially and what that means for the city.

This site is aimed at urban policymakers and city administrators evaluating short-term rental regulation, and at journalists and informed members of the public interested in the housing and tourism dynamics behind Airbnb's growth.

What we set out to answer

T1

Assess market concentration

How are listings distributed across hosts? Does a small number of operators control a disproportionate share of supply?

T2

Classify host types

Distinguish casual (1 listing), semi-professional (2 to 4), and professional hosts (5 or more) and measure their respective market shares.

T3

Compare cities

Identify structural differences between Rome and Copenhagen across price, availability, and host concentration.

T4

Analyse spatial distribution

Where within each city are listings and professional hosts geographically concentrated?

T5

Evaluate urban pressure

Measure neighbourhood-level intensity through a composite Urban Pressure Index combining listing density and year-round availability.

Data

Two cities, one platform

Both datasets come from Inside Airbnb, a public transparency project that scrapes and publishes Airbnb listing data. September 2025 snapshots were selected for both cities to ensure comparability. Listings with missing prices and extreme price outliers above the 99th percentile were removed. Because a large share of Copenhagen listings had no price recorded, price was excluded from the Urban Pressure Index to keep comparisons fair across both cities.

How we measure urban pressure

The Urban Pressure Index (UPI) is a composite metric that measures the relative intensity of Airbnb activity across neighbourhoods. It combines two components, each normalised separately within each city, so that the index captures relative pressure within a city rather than absolute differences between them:

UPI = 0.5 x listing countnorm + 0.5 x availabilitynorm

Equal weights reflect a neutral methodological assumption: in the absence of strong evidence that listing density matters more than availability or vice versa, both contribute equally. A robustness check including normalised price produces similar neighbourhood rankings, giving confidence that the exclusion does not distort the findings.

Which neighbourhoods face the most pressure?
All 26 neighbourhoods ranked by their Urban Pressure Index score. Copenhagen's Indre By tops the chart, but Rome's I Centro Storico follows close behind, and Rome accounts for the bulk of the mid-table. This ranking is the starting point for identifying where targeted regulation is most needed.
Where professional hosts have taken hold
These maps show the share of listings managed by professional hosts in each neighbourhood. In Rome, commercial operators are spread across the entire city with the historic centre darkest. In Copenhagen, professional concentration is almost entirely confined to the city core. The contrast is striking and has direct implications for where housing pressure is being created.
Mapping urban pressure across both cities
The Urban Pressure Index translated onto a map. Red neighbourhoods face the most intense Airbnb activity relative to their city. In Rome, pressure is spread broadly across many districts. In Copenhagen, it is tightly concentrated near the centre. Each city requires a different regulatory response.
Do higher prices signal higher pressure?
Each point is a neighbourhood, plotted by its average nightly price and its UPI score. Neighbourhoods with higher average prices also tend to face higher urban pressure. Copenhagen's Indre By stands out at the top right. The relationship is not causal, but it is consistent and worth monitoring for any regulator tracking market trends.

Ethics

How data visualisation can mislead, and how to spot it

As a policymaker or journalist reading data about short-term rentals, you will encounter charts that appear to prove strong causal relationships between professionalisation and housing pressure. Some of those charts will be produced honestly. Others will use legitimate design choices, no false numbers, no fabricated data, to create an impression that the data cannot actually support. The two visualisations below show how the same underlying dataset produces two very different stories depending on those choices.

Toggle between them to see how easily a chart can mislead without falsifying a single data point. Learning to read these signals is part of interpreting data responsibly.

White hat: honest representation
Scatter plot with confidence intervals, correlation statistics, and a causal disclaimer. Both cities shown.
Why this is white hat: Both cities are shown simultaneously, preventing selective presentation of the stronger result. Both axes are anchored at zero. 95% confidence intervals are shaded around each regression line, making uncertainty visible. Pearson correlation coefficients and p-values are annotated directly on the chart. The caption states explicitly that correlation does not imply causation and identifies the UPI as an author-constructed composite rather than an official statistic. A reader who looks carefully walks away with an accurate picture of both the relationship and its limits.

Conclusion

What this means for cities

The evidence is consistent across every metric. Rome's Airbnb market has undergone a structural shift away from occasional home-sharing toward commercial operation by a small class of professional operators. Copenhagen has not. The implications are different in each city, and so should the policy response be.

1

In Rome, a small operator class controls the majority of supply

Professional hosts represent just 5.1% of all Rome hosts but control 29.4% of listings. Add semi-professional hosts and multi-listing operators together control 58.4% of the entire market. The top 10% of hosts account for 39.2% of supply, and the top 1% alone hold 13.9%. Rome's Gini coefficient of 0.39 confirms that listing ownership is substantially unequal.

2

Copenhagen's platform still functions as originally intended

96% of Copenhagen hosts manage a single listing, and casual hosts control 85.3% of all supply. The top 10% of hosts account for just 20.0% of listings, and the top 1% control 9.1%. The Gini coefficient of 0.11 reflects a broadly equitable distribution. This suggests that tighter regulation can preserve the peer-to-peer character of the platform.

3

Availability reveals commercial intensity, not just tourism seasonality

A listing available 260 days a year is not a holiday home used occasionally. It is a commercial property. Rome's median availability of 260 days against Copenhagen's 115 days, and 68.1% of Rome listings available for six months or more compared to 38.4% in Copenhagen, point to structural differences in how the platform is being used, not just differences in tourist demand.

4

Pressure concentrates in historic districts, not across entire cities

Urban pressure is spatially selective. In Rome, I Centro Storico has both the highest UPI score and the highest professional host share at 38.8%. In Copenhagen, Indre By and Vesterbro-Kongens Enghave record the most relative pressure. This matters for regulation: city-wide rules may be too blunt. Neighbourhood-level data supports targeted intervention where pressure is highest.

5

Professional hosting and urban pressure co-occur, but causation is unproven

Neighbourhoods with higher professional host shares also tend to show higher UPI scores. The Pearson correlation is 0.55 in Rome (p = 0.035) and 0.73 in Copenhagen (p = 0.010). This is not causal evidence. Tourism demand, urban centrality, and housing stock characteristics may drive both simultaneously. But the spatial co-occurrence is consistent, statistically significant, and policy-relevant regardless of direction.

Limitations and next steps

Missing prices

41% of Copenhagen listings had no price recorded. Dropping these rows may introduce selection bias and limits direct price comparisons between cities.

Single snapshot

Both datasets reflect September 2025 only. Seasonal availability and pricing patterns are not captured. A longitudinal dataset would allow structural trends to be separated from seasonal variation.

No housing context

The UPI measures Airbnb activity intensity, not housing displacement directly. Incorporating population density and residential housing supply would allow the index to be reinterpreted as a measure of housing stress.

Platform data only

Inside Airbnb captures only publicly listed properties. Off-platform and unlisted rentals are not reflected, potentially underestimating the true scale of commercial short-term rental activity.
